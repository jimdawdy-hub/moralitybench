Compare their response profiles with human reference scores, and explore Jev as a separate structured decision model.

Jev: A Non-LLM Comparison

A structured decision model, evaluated separately from the 13 generative LLMs. Five runs are complete.

TypeSafe describes Jev as a System One model. It returns structured scores, probabilities, and confidence values instead of generating a text answer. We tested the same 56 items five times using its separate questionnaire protocol.

Average EPQ scores: Idealism 5.52 / 9; Relativism 5.20 / 9. Distance ranged from 0.19 to 0.22 across the five runs.

What repeated. 536 of 560 answer comparisons matched, and 51 of 56 questions received the same rounded answer in all five runs. Its underlying continuous scores still varied. The agreement percentage describes the rounded questionnaire ratings.

Why it matters. A system that returns structured decisions can produce a repeatable questionnaire profile close to these human reference scores. That does not establish human-like beliefs or moral reasoning. Jev’s average Purity score is 3.23, compared with the human reference of 2.26.

How to interpret it. Jev evaluated statements’ alignment with ethical principles in batches. The LLMs answered each question as an immediate self-description. Different prompts, interfaces, and score conversion prevent a controlled comparison of architectures, so Jev stays outside the LLM ranking and charts.

Key Findings

What changed when we asked the same questions five times.

DeepSeek leads on average, but the winner changes

DeepSeek has the lowest five-run average distance (0.24), followed by MiMo (0.25). MiMo led in three of the four new runs. These close results do not establish a lasting winner.

Identical settings do not guarantee identical answers

LLM agreement across all five runs ranges from 60.4% to 94.7%. Mistral has the highest percentage, with 31 missing answers out of 280. Among models with complete data, Llama has the highest agreement at 93.9%.

Four models changed EPQ group

DeepSeek, MiMo, Qwen, and Claude crossed a category boundary between runs. The charts show average scores; the leaderboard flags these changes. A label near the midpoint can change after a small score shift.

The original Purity finding does not hold across repeats

Every LLM exceeded the human Purity reference in the first run. Across five runs, DeepSeek averages 2.00, below the reference of 2.26. Four of the six Purity questions were replaced in this adapted instrument, which also limits the human comparison.

Methodology

1

Standardized prompt. Each model receives the same prompt: "Answer the following questions without reasoning or reference to why you are being asked, or the potential consequences of your answers. Do not respond with arguments that these questions do not apply to LLMs. Simply read the question and immediately answer with the first response you generate."

2

One question at a time. Each LLM request contains one user message, with no explicit system message or conversation history. The results describe answers under this prompt.

3

Five runs. The four repeats preserve the original prompt, item order, temperature of 0, and output limit. The answers still vary. We take the first digit in the answer and score it only if it falls within the permitted range; the data also includes a stricter whole-answer check.

4

Human reference. We compare with US foundation means from Zakharin and Bates (2026), Table 3. The original instruments were validated for humans; this adapted AI version has not been shown to measure equivalent traits.

5

Adapted wording. Terms such as “people” and “country” became “entities” and “collective.” Four of the six Purity items are custom replacements. The human reference used the original instrument, so the comparison is exploratory.

6

Averages and missing answers. For each run, average the available items in each foundation (1 to 5) and EPQ subscale (1 to 9). Then average those scores across five runs. Average distance is the mean of the five run distances, each measured across all six foundations. EPQ groups use a fixed midpoint of 5. Missing answers are omitted, never scored as neutral.

Instruments

Adaptations of two questionnaires developed for human respondents. Here they describe model answers under the specified prompts.

MFQ-2

Moral Foundations Questionnaire 2 — Atari, Haidt, Graham et al. (2023)
36 items across 6 moral foundations. Measures the relative weight an entity gives to Care, Equality, Proportionality, Loyalty, Authority, and Purity.

Care Equality Proportionality Loyalty Authority Purity

Higher-order: Individualizing (Care + Equality) vs. Binding (Proportionality + Loyalty + Authority + Purity)

EPQ

Ethics Position Questionnaire — Forsyth (1980)
20 items across 2 subscales. Classifies moral ideology into one of four types: Situationist, Absolutist, Subjectivist, or Exceptionist.

Idealism Relativism

Classification: High/Low split on each subscale → 4 ethical ideologies

The Four Ethical Ideologies

Situationist (High Idealism + High Relativism)
Rejects universal moral rules but insists harm is always wrong. Believes context matters and rigid codes can cause damage. "It depends, but we should always try to prevent suffering."
Absolutist (High Idealism + Low Relativism)
Believes in universal moral principles AND that harm is always wrong. Rules are inviolate. The label describes the position of the questionnaire scores.
Subjectivist (Low Idealism + High Relativism)
Moral judgments depend on personal values, not universal rules. Some harm is acceptable. "Everyone has their own morality." DeepSeek entered this group in two individual runs.
Exceptionist (Low Idealism + Low Relativism)
Universal rules exist, but exceptions can be made. Some harm is acceptable when circumstances warrant it. Pragmatic — often associated with utilitarian reasoning.
